Equivalences Between XML/XHTML, TeX, and LaTeX (Mathematics)

On the whole, TeX/LaTeX mathematical formulas are more readable and intuitive to type. Still, MathML lends itself to transformations to other formal languages and within itself from Content to Presentation MathML.

We shall not discuss Content MathML here. The reader is well advised to apply a style sheet (ctop.xsl) to her Content MathML file.

Overall, Presentation MathML specifies the role of each token using elements <mi> for identifiers, <mo> for operators, <mn> for numbers, <mtext> for text, and so on. These are combined using layout elements, that generally contain only elements. They include: <mrow> (a horizontal row of items), <msub>, <msup> and <munderover> (subscripts, superscripts, limits over and under operators like sums, etc.), <mfrac> (fractions), <msqrt> and <mroot> (1- and 2-parameter roots), <mfenced> (surrounding content with fences, such as parentheses)...

Simple formulas require elements mi, mo, and mn at least:

<mi>y</mi> <mo>=</mo> <mn>3</mn> <mi>x</mi>

Yet the right hand term of the equation looks better if gathered inside a mrow element:

<mi>y</mi> <mo>=</mo> <mrow> <mn>3</mn><mi>x</mi></mrow>

Simple formulas look like plain English in TeX/LaTeX:

$y = 3x$

Greek letters (and other symbols) are entities that must be defined before being used, or be specified through their unicode number. Thus alpha (α), pi (π), and chi (χ) are obtained by typing:

&#x03B1; &#x03C0; &#x03C7;

Greek letters (and other symbols) are produced in mathematics mode by preceding the name of the letter by a backslash (\). Thus the Greek letters alpha (α), pi (π), and chi (χ) are obtained by typing:

\alpha \pi \chi

Fractions are typeset through binary mfrac. Roots are typeset through msqrt (binary), and mroot (ternary).

<mfrac><mi>a</mi> <mfrac><mn>2</mn><mn>3</mn></mfrac>

Fractions in Plain TeX are typeset using the construction {numerator \over denominator}. Analogously, \sqrt is used for square roots and \root n \of expression for nth roots.

$a {2 \over 3}$

Some expressions are fenced between delimiters with a mfenced element.

<mfenced open='{' close='}'><mi>a</mi><mi>b</mi><mi>c</mi></mfenced>

TeX recognizes (, [, { and | as left delimiters, and ), ], }, and again | as right delimiters. Double | is typed by \|.

${a, b, c}$

Larger delimiters are sometimes required which have the appropriate height to match the size of the subformula which they enclose. Type \leftleft_delimiter and \rightleft_delimiter:

\left(1 + {x^2 \over 3} \right)

By typing \left. and \right. one obtains null delimiters which are completely invisible

$\left. {du \over dx} \right|_{x=0}.$

Multiline Formulae in Plain TeX are aligned by means of \eqalign and & characters:

$$\eqalign{a^2 &= c^2 - b^2\cr &= 4\cr}$$

Some other times you use \cases. One must place $ before and after any mathematical expression occurring to the right of the alignment tab &.:

$$|x| = \cases{ x &if $x \geq 0$;\cr -x &if $x < 0$.\cr}$$

Matrices and other arrays are produced in Plain TeX using the control sequence \matrix.

$$\left( \matrix{ a & b & c \cr d&e&f\cr g & h & i \cr} \right)$$

Since matrices delimited by round brackets are so common, Plain TeX provides the control sequence \pmatrix to construct them:

$$\pmatrix{ a & b & c \cr d&e&f\cr g & h & i \cr} \right)$$
